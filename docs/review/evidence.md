### Taxonomy and initial breadth

### Breadth supplement for the artificial-life review

Checked 2026-11-15. This bounded supplement adds three sources to the digital-evolution material. It distinguishes a field review used for taxonomy from two original experimental papers. The interpretive boundaries below are review synthesis, not claims that any example implements complete artificial life.

**Scope and taxonomy.** Wendy Aguilar, Guillermo Santamaría-Bonfil, Tom Froese, and Carlos Gershenson (2014), “The Past, Present, and Future of Artificial Life,” *Frontiers in Robotics and AI* **1**, article 8. DOI: [10.3389/frobt.2014.00008](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2014.00008). [Publisher full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2014.00008/full); [publisher PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2014.00008/pdf). Read §§2.1-2.4, especially the explicit classification in §2.1. The review treats artificial life as a synthetic investigation of life and life-like processes, spanning overlapping computational, hardware, and biochemical approaches. “Soft” ALife constructs digital systems; “hard” ALife constructs physical machines; “wet” ALife constructs biochemical systems. These classify methods, not stages on a ladder toward life. Soft systems can test consequences of explicit rules through controlled interventions and replay; their outcomes do not independently establish biochemical feasibility. The review also separates individual autonomy from population evolution. Its taxonomy is useful framing, but its 2014 assessment should not be presented as a current survey of accomplishments.

**Embodiment and evolutionary robotics.** Hod Lipson and Jordan B. Pollack (2000), “Automatic design and manufacture of robotic lifeforms,” *Nature* **406**, 974-978. DOI: [10.1038/35023115](https://doi.org/10.1038/35023115). [Publisher record](https://www.nature.com/articles/35023115); [author-hosted manuscript PDF](https://web.mit.edu/people/hlipson/papers/design.pdf). Read manuscript pp. 2-8, Methods (“Evolution process” and “Simulation”), and Table I; the manuscript has 15 PDF pages, unlike the five-page journal typesetting. Morphology and neural control evolved together in simulation under an explicit locomotion fitness function. Selected bodies were fabricated, motors inserted, and physical behavior compared with simulation. This tests whether an evolutionary design process can exploit body-controller interactions and produce functioning embodied artifacts. Table I records substantial distance mismatches for two examples, attributed to slipping and an imperfect friction model. It does not establish robot populations reproducing autonomously in their physical environment: evaluation and selection were simulated, manufacturing infrastructure was supplied, and motors were installed manually. This is a clear hard/soft hybrid, useful for explaining the simulation-to-reality boundary rather than endogenous ecological evolution.

**Bottom-up protocell construction.** Ting F. Zhu and Jack W. Szostak (2009), “Coupled Growth and Division of Model Protocell Membranes,” *Journal of the American Chemical Society* **131**(15), 5705-5713. DOI: [10.1021/ja900919c](https://doi.org/10.1021/ja900919c). [Publisher source](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/ja900919c); [PMC primary-text copy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2669828/). Read primary-source indexed full-text passages from Results (“Vesicle Growth and Division,” “Cycles of Growth and Division”) and Discussion; direct page opening encountered access challenges, and a complete PDF was not inspected. Feeding fatty-acid vesicles with micelles produced elongated compartments that divided under modest shear; encapsulated RNA could pass into daughter compartments. This tests physical compatibility among membrane growth, division, and retention of internal material. It does not demonstrate an internally replicating genome or a complete evolving protocell. The authors explicitly identify genome replication and inheritance as additional requirements. Laboratory feeding and agitation also remain part of the experimental environment; division alone is insufficient evidence of autonomy or open-ended evolution.

Together these examples support a precise review structure: organize substrates separately from the properties demonstrated, and distinguish externally specified performance, material self-maintenance, reproduction, heredity, and continuing adaptive innovation. No experiments were reproduced for this supplement. Two PDF URLs were verified directly; no verified wet-paper PDF URL is supplied.

### Cellular automata

### Cellular-automata foundations supplement

Accessed 2026-11-15. Literature synthesis only; no simulations or kernel-rule changes.

**Discrete self-reproduction.** Christopher G. Langton, “Self-reproduction in cellular automata,” *Physica D* **10**(1-2), 135-144 (1984), DOI [10.1016/0167-2789(84)90256-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-2789(84)90256-2). The [original article scan](https://fab.cba.mit.edu/classes/MAS.865/topics/self_replication/Langton.pdf), hosted by MIT CBA, was read at printed pp. 137-143, especially section 2 and figures 8-10. Langton separates actively directed reproduction from configurations passively duplicated by the transition rules. Circulating signals both direct construction and are copied into offspring. Universal construction is sufficient but unnecessary for this example. The engineered loop produces an exact offspring after 151 updates; local crowding changes reproductive opportunity. This establishes a compact construction mechanism, not spontaneous origin, inherited diversification, or open-ended evolution.

**Inherited change requires additional evidence.** The existing cached primary source, Hiroki Sayama, “Spontaneous evolution of self-reproducing loops on cellular automata,” *InterJournal*, Complex Systems, article 236 (1998), provides that contrast ([institutional PDF](https://necsi.edu/s/sayama-gj26.pdf); cached chapter-formatted copy, sections 1.3-1.5). Modified rules tolerate structural variation; collisions alter offspring organization and consequently their circulating information. Smaller viable loops spread through faster reproduction. Thus deterministic dynamics can generate inherited variation without an externally sampled mutation operator. The reported convergence toward the smallest loops also prevents equating this result with sustained novelty. This 1998 item must remain distinct from Sayama's 1999 *Artificial Life* article and the 2024 retrospective.

**Continuous-valued organization.** Bert Wang-Chak Chan, “Lenia: Biology of Artificial Life,” *Complex Systems* **28**(3), 251-286 (2019), DOI [10.25088/ComplexSystems.28.3.251](https://doi.org/10.25088/ComplexSystems.28.3.251); [publisher PDF](https://content.wolfram.com/sites/13/2019/10/28-3-1.pdf). Relevant full text: introduction, pp. 251-252; definitions/local rule, sections 2.1.2-2.1.7; discovery methods, section 2.2. Lenia generalizes binary CA using extended weighted neighborhoods, a growth mapping, and incremental bounded-state updates. Numerical simulations use finite resolutions; a continuous limit is formulated separately. Diverse persistent and moving patterns show what richer substrates can organize. However, the 2019 introduction explicitly identifies self-replication as undiscovered. “Evolving new species” includes human-guided parameter/configuration mutation and selection for survival, stability, aesthetics, and novelty. Catalogued morphological diversity therefore does not demonstrate an autonomous reproducing population with evolving inheritance. This is a statement about the 2019 paper, not all subsequent Lenia variants.

**Optimized morphogenesis.** Alexander Mordvintsev, Ettore Randazzo, Eyvind Niklasson, and Michael Levin, “Growing Neural Cellular Automata,” *Distill* (11 February 2020), DOI [10.23915/distill.00023](https://doi.org/10.23915/distill.00023); [original interactive web article](https://distill.pub/2020/growing-ca/). Read “Model” and experiments 1-3. A shared neural update rule transforms continuous cell-state vectors using local perception. Backpropagation through time optimizes a pixel loss against a specified target. Reusing intermediate states encourages persistence; including damaged states improves regeneration. Some untrained damage responses also occur, with variation among trained models. These are significant demonstrations of decentralized development and repair, but optimization happens in an external training procedure. The article does not establish autonomous reproduction, inherited rule mutation, or ecological selection during deployment. Its biological vocabulary should not obscure this separation between a learned developmental rule and an evolving population.

**Review implication and limits.** Treat self-reproduction, heritable population change, and optimized morphology as separate achievements. Local rules and lifelike appearances do not establish all three. Relevant original text was read, but software, numerical convergence, and claimed generalization were not independently reproduced. Distill remains a web source; no browser-printed file should be represented as its original PDF. The two new original-PDF endpoints are listed separately for caching; neither is yet assumed present in the repository.
